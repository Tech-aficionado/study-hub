📅 Budget & Budgetary Control Intermediate

Planning ahead in numbers, then checking reality against the plan.

1 What a budget actually is

A budget is a formal, quantitative plan for a future period, expressed mainly in financial terms — expected sales, costs, cash flows, and profit — prepared in advance of the period it covers. It converts a business's goals into specific, measurable numbers that every department can plan around.

Budgetary control is the broader management process built around that budget: continuously comparing actual results against the budgeted figures, analyzing the differences ("variances"), and taking corrective action where needed.

2 Objectives of budgeting

  • Planning — forces management to think ahead systematically about revenue, costs, and resources, rather than reacting as events occur.
  • Coordination — aligns the plans of different departments (sales, production, purchasing, finance) so they support one goal instead of working against each other.
  • Control — gives a clear benchmark to measure actual performance against, making it easy to spot where things are going off-plan.
  • Communication — translates top-level goals into concrete targets every department and employee can understand and work toward.
  • Motivation — a well-set, achievable budget target can motivate teams to perform efficiently.
  • Resource allocation — helps decide how limited funds should be distributed among competing needs.

3 Types of budgets

TypeWhat it means
Fixed BudgetPrepared for one single, specific level of activity/output and NOT adjusted even if actual activity differs. Simple, but less useful for comparison if volumes change.
Flexible BudgetPrepared for several levels of activity (e.g. 70%, 80%, 100% capacity), so it can be adjusted to match whatever the actual activity level turns out to be — giving a fairer basis for variance analysis.
Zero-Based Budget (ZBB)Built from scratch ("zero") every period — every expense must be freshly justified, instead of simply adjusting last year's figures. Prevents unnecessary costs from being carried forward by habit.
Master BudgetThe single, summarized budget that consolidates ALL of a business's individual functional budgets (sales, production, purchases, cash, etc.) into one overall plan — usually presented as a budgeted P&L and budgeted Balance Sheet.
💡 A flexible budget is especially useful for performance evaluation: if actual output was 90% of planned capacity, you compare actuals against the budget RECAST for 90% — not against a plan drawn up for 100%.

4 The budgetary control process

  1. Set objectives for the budget period, aligned with overall business goals.
  2. Prepare the budget — each department drafts its functional budget, consolidated into the master budget.
  3. Record actual performance as the period unfolds.
  4. Compare actual vs. budgeted figures and calculate variances (favorable or adverse).
  5. Investigate significant variances and take corrective action, feeding lessons into the next budget cycle.

5 Advantages and limitations

✅ Advantages

  • Forces forward planning instead of reactive management
  • Improves coordination across departments
  • Provides a clear yardstick to measure performance
  • Helps identify problem areas early through variance analysis
  • Supports better, more informed resource allocation

⚠️ Limitations

  • Based on estimates/forecasts — can go wrong if assumptions are poor
  • Can become rigid; a fixed budget resists changing conditions
  • Time-consuming and costly to prepare properly
  • May create unhealthy departmental competition over allocations
  • Risk of being treated as a target to "just meet," discouraging extra effort
