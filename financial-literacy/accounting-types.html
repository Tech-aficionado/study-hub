⚖️ Financial, Cost & Management Accounting Beginner

Three branches of accounting, three different audiences, three different jobs.

1 Why three branches exist

"Accounting" is not one single activity — it splits into three branches because different people need the numbers for different reasons. A bank deciding whether to lend money needs something different from a factory manager deciding whether a product line is worth keeping. Over time this gave rise to three distinct disciplines, each with its own rules, audience, and time horizon.

2 Financial Accounting

Financial Accounting records, classifies, and summarizes a business's transactions to produce the final statements covered earlier — the Trading & P&L Account and the Balance Sheet — primarily for people outside day-to-day management: shareholders, lenders, tax authorities, and regulators.

  • Objective: show the overall financial position and performance of the whole business for a past period.
  • Scope: the entire organization as one unit; historical data only (what already happened).
  • Governed by: statutory rules and accounting standards (so different companies' statements stay comparable).
  • Frequency: typically annual (with quarterly reporting for listed companies).

3 Cost Accounting

Cost Accounting focuses specifically on recording, classifying, and analyzing the cost of producing a product or delivering a service — materials, labor, and overheads — to help control costs and set prices.

  • Objective: ascertain the cost per unit/product/process and help control and reduce it.
  • Scope: individual products, jobs, processes, or departments — much more granular than financial accounting.
  • Governed by: internal costing methods and conventions (less rigid than financial accounting's statutory rules).
  • Frequency: continuous / as frequently as management needs it.

4 Management Accounting

Management Accounting is the broadest of the three: it takes data from both financial and cost accounting (and other sources — market data, forecasts, non-financial information) and turns it into reports designed purely to help managers plan, decide, and control — budgets, variance reports, break-even analysis, and the kind of marginal-costing decisions covered later in this course.

  • Objective: support internal decision-making and future planning — not just reporting on the past.
  • Scope: whatever slice of the business a specific decision needs — a product, a department, a proposed new investment.
  • Governed by: no external rules at all — reports are designed however management finds most useful.
  • Frequency: as often as decisions need to be made — daily, weekly, or ad hoc.
💡 Easy way to remember the chain: Cost Accounting feeds detailed cost data UP into Management Accounting, which blends it with financial and market data to help managers decide what to do NEXT. Financial Accounting, by contrast, looks BACKWARD and reports OUTWARD.
