📈 Marginal Costing & Managerial Decisions Advanced

Separating fixed from variable cost to find the break-even point — and to make real pricing and capacity decisions.

1 What marginal costing means

Marginal costing is a costing technique that separates total cost into two categories by behavior:

  • Fixed costs — stay the same in total regardless of how many units are produced (rent, salaried staff, depreciation).
  • Variable costs — change in direct proportion to the number of units produced (raw materials, direct labor, per-unit shipping).

Only variable cost is treated as the cost of a unit under this technique; fixed cost is treated as a cost of the period as a whole, charged fully against that period's profit rather than spread across units. This split is what makes marginal costing so useful for short-term decisions.

2 Contribution — the key building block

Contribution (or Contribution Margin) is the amount left from sales revenue after covering variable costs — the amount that "contributes" first toward covering fixed costs, and once fixed costs are covered, toward profit.

Contribution = Sales − Variable Cost
Contribution per unit = Selling Price per unit − Variable Cost per unit
Profit = Total Contribution − Fixed Cost

3 Break-even analysis

The Break-Even Point (BEP) is the level of sales (in units or in rupees) at which total revenue exactly equals total cost — the business makes neither a profit nor a loss. Below it, the business loses money; above it, every additional unit sold adds pure contribution to profit (since fixed costs are already covered).

BEP (in units) = Fixed Cost ÷ Contribution per unit

BEP (in ₹ sales value) = Fixed Cost ÷ P/V Ratio
   where P/V Ratio (Profit-Volume Ratio) = Contribution ÷ Sales × 100
💡 Worked example: A company sells a product at ₹500 per unit. Variable cost is ₹300 per unit. Fixed costs for the period are ₹8,00,000.
Contribution per unit = ₹500 − ₹300 = ₹200
BEP (units) = ₹8,00,000 ÷ ₹200 = 4,000 units

P/V Ratio = (₹200 ÷ ₹500) × 100 = 40%
BEP (sales value) = ₹8,00,000 ÷ 40% = ₹20,00,000

So the company must sell 4,000 units (worth ₹20,00,000) just to cover its costs. If it sells 5,000 units, the extra 1,000 units each contribute ₹200 straight to profit: 1,000 × ₹200 = ₹2,00,000 profit.

4 The break-even chart

The Fixed Cost line is flat because fixed costs don't change with volume. The Total Cost line starts at the fixed-cost level and rises with variable cost per unit. The Total Revenue line starts at zero and rises with the selling price per unit. Where Total Revenue crosses Total Cost is the break-even point — to its left, cost exceeds revenue (a loss); to its right, revenue exceeds cost (a profit).

5 Cost-Volume-Profit (CVP) analysis

CVP analysis is the broader study of how changes in costs, selling price, and sales volume affect a business's profit. It uses the same building blocks as break-even analysis — contribution, fixed cost, P/V ratio — but extends them to answer "what if" questions: What profit results from a given sales volume? What volume is needed for a target profit?

Sales (units) for a Target Profit = (Fixed Cost + Target Profit) ÷ Contribution per unit

Example: Target profit = ₹4,00,000 (same data as above)
Required units = (₹8,00,000 + ₹4,00,000) ÷ ₹200 = 6,000 units

Margin of Safety is another key CVP measure — the gap between actual (or budgeted) sales and the break-even sales, showing how much sales can fall before the business starts losing money.

Margin of Safety = Actual Sales − Break-even Sales

6 Managerial decisions using marginal costing

Because marginal costing isolates each unit's variable cost and contribution, it becomes the standard tool for several classic short-term decisions:

  • Make-or-buy decisions — compare the variable cost of making a component in-house against the price of buying it from an outside supplier. As long as fixed costs are unaffected either way, the cheaper variable cost generally wins.
  • Accept or reject a special/bulk order — even an order priced below normal selling price can be worth accepting if it still covers its variable cost and contributes something toward fixed cost, provided spare capacity exists and it doesn't disturb regular sales.
  • Key/limiting factor decisions — when a scarce resource (machine hours, raw material, labor hours) restricts production, products should be ranked and prioritized by contribution per unit of the limiting factor, not by contribution per unit alone.
  • Discontinuing a product line — a product showing an "accounting loss" after allocating fixed overheads may still be worth keeping if it earns positive contribution, since dropping it would only remove that contribution while fixed costs mostly remain.
  • Pricing decisions — in the short run, any price above variable cost adds some contribution; marginal costing helps managers see the floor below which a price should not normally be set.
⚠️ Marginal costing is a short-term decision tool. It assumes fixed costs genuinely stay fixed and that cost behavior is linear — assumptions that break down over longer time horizons or outside the "relevant range" of activity.
