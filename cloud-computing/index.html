☁️ What is Cloud Computing? Beginner

Definition, history, architecture, cloud types, business models and the open-source platforms that shaped the field.

1 Definition

Cloud computing is a model for delivering on-demand computing resources — servers, storage, databases, networking, software — over the internet, with pay-as-you-go pricing instead of owning and maintaining physical hardware. NIST's widely-cited definition calls out five essential characteristics: on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity, and measured service.

💡 Think of it like electricity: you don't own a power plant in your backyard — you plug in and pay only for what you use. Cloud computing does the same for compute and storage.

2 Benefits

  • Cost efficiency — no upfront capital expense on hardware; pay only for consumed resources (CapEx → OpEx).
  • Elastic scalability — scale compute up during a traffic spike and back down afterward, automatically.
  • Global reach — deploy close to users across regions in minutes, not months of procurement.
  • Reliability — providers run geographically redundant data centers with built-in failover.
  • Managed maintenance — the provider patches, upgrades and secures the underlying infrastructure.
  • Faster time-to-market — spin up a database or a VM in minutes instead of weeks of hardware procurement.

3 A short history

  • 1960s — John McCarthy proposes that computing could one day be organized as a public utility, like electricity.
  • 1999 — Salesforce pioneers delivering enterprise applications over the web (early SaaS).
  • 2002 — Amazon launches Amazon Web Services, initially exposing storage and compute as web services.
  • 2006 — AWS launches EC2 and S3, widely seen as the start of modern commercial IaaS.
  • 2008–2010 — Google App Engine and Microsoft Azure launch, open-sourcing frameworks like Eucalyptus appear.
  • 2010s onward — containers, Kubernetes, serverless and multi-cloud strategies mature the ecosystem.

4 Cloud architecture

A cloud system is generally split into a front end (what the client/user interacts with — browser, app) and a back end (the provider's actual infrastructure: servers, storage, virtualization layer, and a management layer that handles scheduling, monitoring and billing), connected over the internet.

6 Business models

  • Pay-per-use — billed strictly by consumption (compute-hours, GB stored, requests served).
  • Subscription — a flat recurring fee for a bundle of capacity or seats (common in SaaS).
  • Freemium — a free tier to drive adoption, with paid tiers for scale or advanced features.
  • Reserved/committed-use — customers commit to usage for a discount versus on-demand pricing.
  • Brokerage — a cloud service broker aggregates and resells multiple providers' services.

8 Open-source cloud platforms & simulators

  • Eucalyptus — open-source software for building AWS-EC2-compatible private/hybrid clouds; one of the earliest open IaaS platforms.
  • Nimbus — an open-source toolkit (grew out of scientific computing/Globus) for turning a cluster into an IaaS cloud, popular in academic/research settings.
  • OpenNebula — an open-source cloud management platform for building private and hybrid clouds, with strong support for both KVM/Xen virtualization and bare-metal.
  • CloudSim — not a cloud itself, but a widely used Java-based simulation toolkit for modeling cloud data centers, resource provisioning policies, and scheduling algorithms for research without real hardware cost.

9 Risks

  • Vendor lock-in — proprietary APIs/services make migrating to another provider costly.
  • Data security & privacy — data sits on third-party infrastructure; breach/compliance exposure shifts but doesn't disappear.
  • Downtime & outages — even major providers have regional outages that ripple to every dependent app.
  • Compliance complexity — data residency and regulatory rules (GDPR, HIPAA) vary by region and industry.
  • Unpredictable cost — pay-as-you-go can spiral without governance (runaway auto-scaling, forgotten resources).
  • Limited control — customers depend on the provider's roadmap, SLAs and incident response.
⚠️ Shared responsibility: the provider secures the cloud's infrastructure; the customer is still responsible for securing what they put in it (identity, data, configuration).
