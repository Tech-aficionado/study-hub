👁️ Image Formation & Camera Calibration Unit 1

How a 3D world becomes a 2D image, and how we recover a camera's own geometry from it.

1 What is computer vision?

Computer vision is the field concerned with giving machines the ability to extract meaning from images and video — detecting objects, reconstructing 3D geometry, tracking motion, recognizing scenes — the inverse problem to computer graphics. Where graphics starts from a 3D scene description and renders a 2D image, vision starts from one or more 2D images and tries to recover the 3D scene, the camera's position, or semantic labels for what is in the picture.

Every vision pipeline begins with the same question: how did this image come to exist? Answering that — the process of image formation — is the foundation for everything downstream: without a model of how a 3D point projects onto a 2D sensor, you cannot undo that projection to recover depth, shape, or camera motion.

2 Geometric camera models

A geometric camera model describes the mathematical mapping from a 3D point in the world to a 2D point on the image plane. The simplest and most instructive model is the pinhole camera: light from a 3D point travels in a straight line through a single infinitesimal aperture (the pinhole) and lands on an image plane behind it, forming an inverted image.

Because a real pinhole lets through too little light, real cameras use a lens instead, focusing a wider cone of rays onto the same point — but the thin-lens model reduces to the same pinhole projection geometry for points near the optical axis, which is why the pinhole model remains the standard abstraction in vision.

3 Orthographic vs. perspective projection

Two idealized projection models describe how 3D points map to 2D:

  • Perspective projection — rays converge at a single center of projection (the pinhole). A point's image coordinates are x = f·X/Z, y = f·Y/Z: objects farther away (larger Z) appear smaller. This is how real cameras and human eyes actually work, and it is why parallel lines appear to converge at a vanishing point.
  • Orthographic projection — rays are parallel (the center of projection is at infinity); a point simply drops its depth coordinate: x = X, y = Y. There is no foreshortening with distance. This is a useful simplification for scenes where depth variation is small relative to the viewing distance (e.g. aerial/satellite imagery, or object recognition where scale is normalized).

4 Weak perspective projection

Weak perspective is a middle ground between the two: it assumes the scene's depth variation is small compared to the average distance from the camera, so every point is projected perspectively using one average depth Z̄ rather than each point's own Z. The projection becomes x = f·X/Z̄, y = f·Y/Z̄ — effectively an orthographic projection followed by a single uniform scale factor f/Z̄.

💡 Weak perspective is popular in structure-from-motion and object-pose algorithms because it keeps the math linear (like orthographic) while staying a reasonable approximation of real perspective when the object is far from the camera relative to its own depth extent.

5 Intrinsic and extrinsic camera parameters

A full camera model splits cleanly into two parameter groups:

  • Intrinsic parameters — describe the camera's own internal geometry, independent of where it is placed in the world: focal length (fx, fy), principal point (cx, cy — where the optical axis meets the image plane), skew between the sensor axes, and lens distortion coefficients. These are packed into the intrinsic (calibration) matrix K:
K = | fx   s   cx |
    | 0   fy   cy |
    | 0    0    1 |
  • Extrinsic parameters — describe where the camera sits in the world: a 3×3 rotation matrix R and a 3×1 translation vector t that transform world coordinates into the camera's own coordinate frame.

The full projection of a 3D world point Xw to a 2D pixel is: x = K [R | t] Xw. Intrinsics are fixed properties of the camera/lens (found once via calibration); extrinsics change every time the camera moves.

6 Camera calibration: linear and non-linear approaches

Camera calibration is the process of estimating K, R, t (and distortion coefficients) from observations of known 3D points and their corresponding 2D image projections — typically a checkerboard pattern photographed from several angles.

  • Linear (direct) methods — e.g. the Direct Linear Transform (DLT). Given enough 3D–2D point correspondences, the projection equation is rearranged into a homogeneous linear system and solved in closed form (via SVD) for the projection matrix. Fast and non-iterative, but it ignores lens distortion entirely and can be sensitive to noise, since it does not minimize a geometrically meaningful error.
  • Non-linear (iterative) methods — take the linear solution as an initial estimate, then refine it by minimizing the actual reprojection error (the pixel distance between observed image points and points reprojected through the current parameter estimate) using an optimizer such as Levenberg–Marquardt. This step can also solve for non-linear lens distortion (radial and tangential), which the linear model cannot represent. Zhang's method (used in OpenCV's calibrateCamera) combines a linear initialization with this non-linear refinement.
Linear methods give you a fast, good starting guess; non-linear refinement is what makes a calibration accurate enough for real 3D reconstruction, because it accounts for the lens distortion that real optics always introduce.
