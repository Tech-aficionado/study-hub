🧩 Normalization: 1NF → BCNF Advanced

One messy table, normalised step by step — so the data can never contradict itself.

1 The problem: one giant table

Imagine tracking course enrollments in a single table. It works until you try to change anything.

studentcoursesinstructorinstructor_office
AnaSQL, PythonDr. LeeB-204
BenSQLDr. LeeB-204
🛑 Three anomalies lurk here: update — move Dr. Lee's office and you must edit every row; miss one and the data disagrees. Insertion — you can't record a new course with no student yet. Deletion — remove the last student of a course and the course+instructor info vanishes.

2 First Normal Form (1NF) — atomic values

Rule: each cell holds a single, atomic value; no repeating groups or comma-lists. Split the courses cell into separate rows.

studentcourseinstructorinstructor_office
AnaSQLDr. LeeB-204
AnaPythonDr. NgC-110
BenSQLDr. LeeB-204
⚠️ A comma-separated list in one column ('SQL, Python') is the most common 1NF violation. It makes "find everyone taking Python" a fragile string search instead of a clean WHERE course = 'Python'.

3 Second Normal Form (2NF) — no partial dependency

Rule: (applies when the key is composite) every non-key column must depend on the whole key, not just part of it. Here the key is (student, course), but instructor depends only on course — a partial dependency. Split it out.

-- enrollments: depends on the full key (student, course)
enrollments(student, course)

-- courses: instructor depends on course alone
courses(course, instructor, instructor_office)

4 Third Normal Form (3NF) — no transitive dependency

Rule: no non-key column depends on another non-key column. In courses, instructor_office depends on instructor, which depends on course — a transitive chain. Break it into its own table.

enrollments(student, course)
courses(course, instructor)             -- instructor depends on course (the key)
instructors(instructor, office)         -- office depends on instructor (its own key)
💡 Now Dr. Lee's office lives in exactly ONE place. Updating it is a single-row change, and the three anomalies are gone. "3NF" is the practical target for most OLTP schemas.

5 Boyce-Codd Normal Form (BCNF) — the stricter 3NF

BCNF tightens 3NF: every determinant (anything that functionally determines another column) must be a candidate key. It differs from 3NF only in rare cases with overlapping candidate keys.

Example: a table (student, subject, tutor) where each tutor teaches exactly one subject, and a student has one tutor per subject. Here tutor → subject, but tutor isn't a key — a BCNF violation. Split into (student, tutor) and (tutor, subject).

The normal forms nest: BCNF ⊂ 3NF ⊂ 2NF ⊂ 1NF. Reaching BCNF means you've satisfied all the lower forms too. Higher forms (4NF for multi-valued dependencies, 5NF) exist but are rarely needed in practice.

6 Denormalization — breaking the rules on purpose

Normalization optimises for correctness and write-efficiency. Sometimes you deliberately denormalize (duplicate data) for read performance — caching an order_count on customers, or a star schema in analytics. The key word is deliberate: a conscious trade of write complexity for read speed, with a plan to keep the copies in sync (triggers, scheduled jobs, or app logic).
